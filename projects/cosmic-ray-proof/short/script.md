# Short script

**Working title:** A Particle From Space Can Flip a Computer Bit  
**Target:** 58–62 seconds, approximately 170 WPM

Your computer can change a zero into a one without a hacker, a software bug, or a
broken chip. Sometimes, the chain begins in space.

A high-energy cosmic ray strikes Earth's atmosphere and creates a shower of secondary
particles. Most pass harmlessly through us. But if one crosses the right part of a
memory chip, it can leave a tiny burst of electrical charge.

That charge may be enough to flip one stored bit: zero becomes one, or one becomes
zero. The transistor still works. The data is simply wrong. Engineers call this a
single-event upset—or a soft error.

In space, satellites face far more radiation. ESA found random memory flips while
studying ten years of its Swarm spacecraft data.

So computers fight invisible particles with error-correcting memory: extra bits that
can detect and repair many mistakes.

The machine did exactly what physics told it to do. It just wasn't what the programmer
wrote.
