But now ask the question the demo cannot answer:
how do we know the agent is right?
A fluent plan can still choose a bad simulation, misunderstand an output,
or carry an error into the next step.
Science can't use confidence as proof.
The reported examples show why checks have to sit inside the loop.
The company says a KTH Royal Institute of
Technology agent repaired broken GROMACS molecular-dynamics setups,
fixing 81% of test cases in one overnight run.
Each fix was independently checked by GROMACS.
That's much stronger than the agent simply claiming success.
The check has to come from the scientific software, a benchmark, a simulation,
or a researcher with the right expertise.
The company's guidance says verification can require computational or
real-world data and may take hours or days.
One wrong assumption can derail the task.
So if you use a system like this, give it a bounded job,
not control of the whole lab.
Define the goal, limit the tools, inspect the output, and run an independent check.
Keep the inputs and logs so someone can reproduce what happened.
That's how a fast assistant becomes useful evidence, rather than a confident guess.
