== PAIR 2: long video, middle of a chapter (same video) ==
DRAFT:
Now ask what happened before a real motor turned.
Engineers built a digital version of the robot and its surroundings,
including joints, limits, sensors, surfaces, walls, and obstacles.
Each simulated robot observes its position, speed, tilt, and surroundings.
It then sends commands to its joints and watches the result.
A better result earns a stronger reward from the training system.
Falling, missing the route, or hitting an obstacle produces a worse result.
When a simulated robot falls, the system resets it immediately,
then sends the same task through another attempt without repairs.
A simulated fall cannot damage a motor, injure a person, or stop training.
OWNER'S VERSION:
The lesson happened before a single real motor turned, inside a computer.
Engineers built a digital copy of the robot and everything around it,
the joints, how far each one can bend, the sensors, the floor, the walls and the obstacles.
The digital robot feels where it is, how fast it's going and how much it's leaning,
it sends commands to its joints, and then it sees what happened.
If it does well, the system gives it a reward, and if it falls, misses the route or hits something, it gets a worse score.
And here's where the computer wins big.
When a digital robot falls, it resets in a split second and tries again, nothing to repair, nobody to pick it up.
In the computer, a robot can fail a thousand times before lunch and nothing breaks.

== PAIR 3: a whole short (Centa Painter, 2026-10-25) ==
DRAFT:
Indian wall finishing used to depend on people for every pass,
but Centa Painter now handles broad repetitive surfaces.
Workers spread putty, sand it smooth, apply primer and paint.
Centa's wheeled base carries a spray tool,
then swaps to a rotary sanding module for walls and ceilings.
A laser measures distance, called LiDAR, while cameras spot windows and switchboards.
Onboard computing builds a local map, so the robot locates itself
and plans coverage without a pre-fed floor plan or fixed path.
The engineering principle is feedback control.
Sensors read the room, the robot moves, checks the pass and adjusts.
People still prepare material, clean the pump and position the machine,
while the final ten centimetres near edges stay manual.
Centa Painter is a modular finishing machine, not a humanoid builder.
The real story is shared work, not builders disappearing overnight.
OWNER'S VERSION:
A robot is painting walls in India now, and it doesn't even need a floor plan.
Finishing a wall there is all hand work,
putty, sanding, primer and paint, done by people.
Centa Painter takes over the big repetitive part.
It rolls around carrying a spray tool,
then swaps to a spinning sander for walls and ceilings.
It finds its way with a laser that measures distance,
and cameras that spot windows and switchboards so it works around them.
From that it maps the room itself and plans how to cover every wall,
and it keeps checking, it sprays, looks at the pass and adjusts.
But it's not doing the whole job.
People still prepare the material, clean the pump and move it into place,
and the last ten centimetres near the edges are still done by hand.
So this isn't a robot builder taking over,
it's a machine that does the big flat walls the same way every time,
while people handle the parts that keep changing.
